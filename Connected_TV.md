# Accessibility and Usability Guidance for Television and Connected TV Platforms

## Purpose and scope

This guide covers accessibility and usability across television receivers and connected TV platforms, including smart TVs, set-top boxes, recorders, and their interfaces. It focuses on how people install and configure devices, control hardware, navigate services, discover and play content, and find and use access services and spoken interfaces.

The guidance applies to the product and platform experience, not to the production of programmes. It addresses two connected areas: the usability of devices and interfaces, and the accessibility features and services available through them.

Complexity is a central access risk. Hybrid TV services may combine network setup, accounts and authentication, several content sources, multiple players, and inconsistent interaction models. These demands can exclude older and disabled users, especially where setup and everyday use depend on technical knowledge, precise input, or memory.

## Design for the whole viewing journey

### 1. Setup and configuration

First-time setup is a high-risk point. People may have to connect a network, create or access accounts, enter text, choose settings, and understand several steps before reaching content.

- Use clear, plain language instead of unexplained technical terms.
- Show progress and explain what the device is doing.
- Make text entry and network configuration accessible through the available input methods.
- Provide spoken feedback where possible.
- Make setup understandable without requiring a manual, while keeping accessible instructions available.

### 2. Navigation and content discovery

People need to move between live TV, on-demand services, apps, programme guides, search, and device settings without losing orientation.

- Use consistent navigation and control patterns across services and players.
- Keep focus and current location clear, including on large screens and when navigating remotely.
- Make content discovery and search usable through spoken output as well as visual presentation.
- Avoid making users relearn basic tasks when they change source or player.

### 3. Live, on-demand, and player consistency

Accessibility breaks when controls behave differently between live television, apps, and on-demand players, or when access-service indicators and activation routes change without explanation.

- Keep navigation, search, playback, and access-service controls as consistent as the service allows.
- Make differences between services clear when identical behaviour is not possible.
- Preserve content and task context when moving between sources or players.

### 4. Remote controls and physical interaction

The remote is a primary input interface, tactile aid, and cognitive support tool. Its physical and on-screen behaviour should work together.

Consider button layout and grouping, tactile identification, spacing, texture, grip, physical feedback, weight, and balance. Provide clear feedback when an action is accepted, and ensure the on-screen focus or state makes the result understandable. See [Physical and Connected Product Control](Physical_and_Connected_Product_Control.md) for broader connected-device and control-system guidance.

### 5. Access services and preferences

Subtitles, captions, audio description, signing, and other available access services are core features of the viewing experience. Availability alone is not enough: people need to discover, select, and use them.

- Clearly indicate which access services are available for content.
- Make controls easy to find and consistent across live and on-demand services.
- Support user preferences and explain when they persist or need to be set again.
- Support searching and filtering for accessible content where the service provides those functions.
- Make it possible to record accessible content and retain relevant service information.

### 6. Spoken output and text-to-speech

Spoken interfaces should provide functional access to the same essential tasks as visual interfaces. Spoken output should cover menus and lists, programme guides, content discovery, context, warnings and prompts, settings, playback, and recording controls.

Do not assume that speech is the only nonvisual route or that every person wants spoken output. Make spoken output controllable and provide clear context and feedback.

### 7. Documentation, packaging, and support

Accessibility begins before the screen is switched on and continues when something goes wrong.

- Provide clear packaging and accessible quick-start and full user guides.
- Offer alternative formats and plain-language instructions.
- Make troubleshooting information realistic, findable, and usable with the available access methods.

## Research and evaluation

Test realistic tasks with disabled people before release, including people who rely on spoken output, people with low vision or who are blind, and people with dexterity impairments. Include setup, search, switching between live and on-demand services, enabling access services, playback, and recovery from errors.

Record the device and software version, service, task, environment, access methods used, and what evidence supports each conclusion. Do not treat an automated review or synthetic scenario as user research or as proof that a product is accessible.

## Agent retrieval guide

For a request about a TV or connected-TV experience, identify the device type, service or player, task, relevant access services, input method, and use context. Retrieve the section that matches the task, then check related sections for setup, remote interaction, spoken output, preferences, and recovery.

Route general modality principles to [Inclusive Multimodal UX](https://github.com/Double-FW/Inclusive-Multimodal-UX), product-control integration to [Physical and Connected Product Control](Physical_and_Connected_Product_Control.md), content production to [Accessible Film, TV and Video Content Production Management](https://github.com/Double-FW/Accessible-Film-TV-and-Video-Content-Production-Management), caption authoring to [Closed Caption Subtitles](https://github.com/Double-FW/Closed-Caption-Subtitles), and regulatory questions to the applicable legal source, including the [AVMSD repository](https://github.com/Double-FW/The-Audiovisual-Media-Services-Directive-AVMSD-).
